1. Introduction

1.1 Background

Every software project begins with requirements: the Software Requirements Specification (SRS) records what the system shall do and to what quality; developers code from it and testers test against it. But requirements are written in imprecise natural language: “The system shall respond quickly” states neither how quickly nor under what load, and “It shall generate reports as needed” leaves “it” and “needed” to the reader’s guess. Defects introduced during the requirements phase are the costliest to repair, since they surface late. Fortunately, much ambiguity follows recognisable patterns — vague quantities, subjective words, optional wording, absolutes, unclear pronouns, unmeasurable claims — which a program can detect automatically.

1.2 Project Overview

SRS Ambiguity Detector is a web application that reviews SRS text and documents for ambiguous, incomplete or unverifiable requirements. A registered user pastes requirement text or uploads a PDF, DOCX or TXT document; the system segments the input into individual requirements, applies a deterministic rule-based engine of eleven ambiguity categories, and presents a report with an overall score, severity counts, category distribution, four health dimensions, and findings showing the flagged phrase, reason, and recommendation. Analyses are saved in PostgreSQL for reopening and trend analysis. An optional enhancement adds an overview and rewrite suggestions via the user’s own encrypted credentials.

1.3 Need for the Project

  • Manual review of a long SRS is slow and inconsistent; careless wording escapes notice and is read differently by developers and testers, causing rework.
  • An explainable, affordable tool is needed that flags the phrase, states the reason, and suggests the rewrite.
  • A repeatable score is needed to compare drafts and track quality over time.

2. Problem Statement and Motivation

2.1 Problem Statement

SRS documents are written in natural language and commonly contain vague, subjective, incomplete and unverifiable statements — “fast response”, “many users”, “as needed”, “user-friendly”. The same statement is interpreted differently by different stakeholders. Since detection depends on slow, inconsistent manual reading, an automated, explainable, repeatable tool is needed to pinpoint such phrases and summarise quality in a trackable score.

2.2 Effects of Ambiguous Requirements

  • Rework and cost overrun: requirement defects surface late, when design, code and tests change together.
  • Incorrect implementation: developers fill gaps with their own assumptions.
  • Unverifiable testing: without measurable criteria, pass/fail cannot be decided objectively.
  • Estimation errors and acceptance disputes: hidden scope (“etc.”, “and/or”) makes estimates unreliable and invites acceptance disputes.

2.3 Motivation

The motivation came from observing time spent arguing over requirement sentences a first review could correct in minutes. Most ambiguity comes from a few careless wording patterns repeated across documents, which a program finds reliably in seconds. A deterministic rule-based approach was chosen: explainable, transparent, independent of external services. Building it as a complete web product provided end-to-end practice in developing and testing real software.

2.5 Success Criteria

  • Pasted text and PDF/DOCX/TXT documents share one analysis pipeline.
  • At least ten ambiguity categories, each with phrase, severity, reason and recommendation.
  • Score, band and health follow a documented, deterministic formula.
  • Users can register, verify, log in, save and search analyses, and view dashboards.
  • No user can access another’s data; the system passes its test suites and a live demonstration.

2.6 Expected Outcomes

  • A working, documented, tested and maintainable SRS ambiguity detection web application.
  • Earlier detection of weak requirements, and a measurable quality indicator (score, band, health) making improvement visible.

3. Objectives and Scope

3.1 Objectives

  1. To develop a web application that reviews SRS text and documents for likely ambiguity.
  2. To implement deterministic segmentation into ordered requirement statements.
  3. To implement eleven rule-based detectors covering vague, subjective, optional, absolute and passive language, measurable criteria, terminology, operators, constraints and completeness.
  4. To attach phrase, offsets, severity, reason and recommendation to every finding, and compute score, bands and health dimensions.
  5. To provide secure accounts (registration, verification, login, password reset, account deletion) and PostgreSQL persistence.
  6. To offer optional enhancement via encrypted user credentials, plus privacy lifecycle features.
  7. To verify the system with automated tests and a live demonstration.

3.2 Scope of the System

3.2.1 In Scope

  • Paste-text analysis up to 200,000 characters; single-document upload (PDF, DOCX, TXT) up to 10 MB with text extraction.
  • Deterministic segmentation, eleven detectors, deduplication, scoring, bands and health dimensions.
  • Email/password authentication with verification, reset, secure cookie sessions and refresh-token rotation.
  • Saved reports, searchable history, dashboard, and optional enhancement with encrypted credentials.
  • Profile, privacy and retention settings; data export and account deletion; responsive interface.

3.2.2 Out of Scope

  • External identity-provider login (OAuth); OCR for scanned PDFs.
  • Analysis of languages other than English; team workspaces and multi-user collaboration.
  • Batch/CI processing. The tool reports patterns that may indicate ambiguity; final judgement remains with the analyst.

3.3 Assumptions

  • SRS content is in English; users have a modern browser and internet connection.
  • A finding indicates a possible ambiguity; a flagged phrase can occasionally be acceptable in context.
  • The optional enhancement needs the user’s own provider credential; the core analysis needs none.
